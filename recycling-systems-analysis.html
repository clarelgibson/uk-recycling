Comparison of Recycling Systems in England

Does the recycling system have a meaningful impact on recycling rates?

Author

Clare Gibson

Introduction

This paper sets out to answer the question: Does the recycling system have a meaningful impact on recycling rates?

Local authorities in England can elect to use one of three collection types for household recycling:

  • Co-Mingled: all non-organic recyclable material is put into a single bin by the householder
  • Two Stream: non-organic recyclable material must be sorted into two bins by the householder (e.g. one bin for glass and metal, and a second bin for paper, card and plastic)
  • Multi-Stream: householders sort their non-organic recyclable material into separate bins for each type of material

Multi-stream systems place a higher burden on the householder, so this paper seeks to investigate whether this higher burden results in higher recycling rates.

Test for significance

The default statistical test would be a one-way ANOVA, which compares group means. But ANOVA assumes each group is roughly normally distributed with similar variance, and our groups are uneven (157 / 112 / 40) and somewhat skewed, as shown above. Instead, we will use the Kruskal-Wallis test: the non-parametric equivalent. It works on the ranks of the values instead of the raw values, so it makes no assumption about the shape of the distribution, and it handles unequal group sizes comfortably. It tests whether at least one group tends to sit higher than the others.

# Run the KW test for recycling rate as explained by collection type
kruskal.test(household_recycle_rate ~ collection_type, data = recycling)

    Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test

data:  household_recycle_rate by collection_type
Kruskal-Wallis chi-squared = 9.8269, df = 2, p-value = 0.007347

The p-value is below 0.05, which indicates a significant effect. However, a p-value only tells us whether there is an effect, not how big it is. With 309 authorities, even a trivial difference can come out significant. Our question asks about a meaningful impact, so we need an effect size, which measures how much of the variation in recycling rates is explained by collection system.

recycling |> 
  kruskal_effsize(household_recycle_rate ~ collection_type)

This test shows us that while collection type does have a real effect on recycling rates, the effect size is small. Finally, we can run a Dunn test to see which of the collection types show a significant difference in terms of recycling rates.

dunn <- recycling |> 
  dunn_test(household_recycle_rate ~ collection_type,
            p.adjust.method = "holm")

Conclusion

Using the 2021 recycling data for English local authorities, we have shown that three different collection systems exist across the 309 local authorities: Co-Mingled, where all recyclable waste goes into a single container; Two Stream, where recyclable waste is sorted into two containers; and Multi-Stream, where recyclable waste must be sorted into at least three different containers. Co-Mingled systems are the most prevalent across England, with over half of local authorities employing this system. Two Stream systems represent a further 36% of local authorities and Multi-Stream systems are employed by just 13% of authorities.

Given the uneven group sizes, we opted to use the the Kruskal-Wallis test to test for significance. With a p-value of <0.05, this test shows that at least one system differs from the other two to a statistically significant degree. Using Dunn’s test, we were able ti identify an interesting result. Co-Mingled systems are significantly worse than multi-stream systems, but two-stream and multi-stream cannot be told apart.
